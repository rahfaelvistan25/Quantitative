Part 1

UNDERSTANDING QUANTITATIVE ANALYSIS

1.1 What is Quantitative Analysis?

Before we discuss complicated formulas, let's understand the meaning of the word.

  • The word Quantitative means something that can be measured, counted, or expressed using numbers.
  • The word Analysis means examining information carefully to understand something or solve a problem.
💡
Core Definition

Quantitative Analysis is the process of using numbers, data, mathematical calculations, and logical methods to understand problems and make decisions.

In simple English: Quantitative Analysis means using numbers to help us make better decisions.

Example 1: Buying a Computer

Imagine you are a student and you want to buy a computer for programming. You have two choices:

Computer Price
Computer A ₱25,000
Computer B ₱30,000

Which computer is cheaper? Of course, Computer A.

Calculation
• ₱30,000 − ₱25,000 = ₱5,000
Computer A is ₱5,000 cheaper than Computer B.

But should you automatically purchase Computer A? Not necessarily!

What if Computer B has better RAM, a faster processor, and more storage? This is where quantitative analysis becomes useful. We do not only compare prices. We also compare specifications and other factors that matter.

📌 Lesson: Numbers help us compare available choices before deciding.

Example 2: Calculating Students' Average Grades

Imagine that a teacher gives five students an examination. Their scores are:

Student Score
Student 180
Student 290
Student 385
Student 475
Student 570

The teacher wants to know the average score of the class.

Step-by-Step Solution
Step 1: Add all scores: 80 + 90 + 85 + 75 + 70 = 400
Step 2: Count the number of students: 5 students
Step 3: Divide total score by number of students: Average = Total Scores ÷ Number of Students = 400 ÷ 5
Final Answer: The class average is 80.

Why did we divide by 5? Because we want to distribute the total score equally across the five students to determine the average. This is a clear, simple example of quantitative analysis.

Example 3: Checking Internet Speed

An IT department wants to know whether its internet connection is sufficient. The laboratory has 40 computers. Each computer is estimated to need 5 Mbps of bandwidth during the planned activity.

Bandwidth Calculation
Step 1: Identify number of computers: 40 computers
Step 2: Identify estimated bandwidth required per computer: 5 Mbps
Step 3: Required Bandwidth = Number of Computers × Bandwidth per Computer = 40 × 5 Mbps
Required Bandwidth = 200 Mbps

Suppose the school only has 100 Mbps.

  • Required bandwidth = 200 Mbps
  • Available bandwidth = 100 Mbps
  • Difference: 200 − 100 = 100 Mbps
⚠️
Network Deficit Detected: The estimated requirement is 100 Mbps greater than available capacity. The school may need to investigate its network usage and consider an upgrade.
Remember: This is an estimate. Actual bandwidth requirements depend on what students are doing online and how many use bandwidth simultaneously.

Part 2

DECISION-MAKING MODELS

2.1 What is Decision-Making?

Decision-making means choosing one option from several available choices. For example:

  • Which laptop should I purchase?
  • Which internet provider should our school use?
  • Which software is better?
  • How many computers should we buy?
  • Which project should our organization prioritize?

Every time we choose between alternatives, we are making a decision. However, some decisions involve money, time, resources, and possible risks. That is why we need a systematic way of choosing.

2.2 What is a Decision-Making Model?

A Decision-Making Model is a simplified representation of a real-world problem that helps us compare options and choose an appropriate solution.

In simple terms: It is a guide that helps us decide what to do by organizing important information.

Example: Choosing an Internet Plan

Imagine that the school needs an internet subscription with two choices:

Information Plan A Plan B
Monthly Fee₱1,500₱2,000
Speed100 Mbps200 Mbps
Contract12 months12 months
Annual Cost Comparison
Plan A: ₱1,500 × 12 = ₱18,000 per year
Plan B: ₱2,000 × 12 = ₱24,000 per year
Diff: ₱24,000 − ₱18,000 = ₱6,000 savings with Plan A
Trade-off: Plan A saves ₱6,000 annually, but Plan B provides 2× the advertised speed.

Now the school must decide whether the extra 100 Mbps is worth the additional ₱6,000 per year. That is the exact purpose of a decision-making model: it makes trade-offs transparent.

2.3 Four Basic Components of a Decision-Making Model

There are four foundational components every student must master:

A. Decision Variables

A decision variable represents something that the decision-maker can control or choose.

Examples: Number of computers to purchase, number of employees to hire, products to manufacture, hours allocated to a project.

Worked Example

Let x = Number of computers to purchase.
Here, x is the decision variable because the school chooses the value: if they choose 10, x = 10; if 15, x = 15.

B. Parameters

Parameters are known, given, or estimated values used in the calculation that the decision-maker does not immediately alter.

Examples: Price of a computer, monthly electricity rate, internet subscription fee, given budget ceiling.

Worked Example

One computer costs ₱25,000 (parameter c). If the school decides to buy 10 computers (x = 10):
Total Cost = x × c = 10 × ₱25,000 = ₱250,000

C. Constraints

Constraints are limitations or restrictions that the solution must obey.

Examples: Limited budget, limited classroom space, limited working hours, hardware maximums.

Worked Example

Budget = ₱300,000; Price per computer = ₱25,000.
Maximum Computers = Budget ÷ Price = ₱300,000 ÷ ₱25,000 = 12 computers

Purchasing 13 computers costs 13 × ₱25,000 = ₱325,000 (exceeds budget by ₱25,000). Hence, the budget is our constraint.

D. Objective Function

An objective function describes what we want to achieve (minimize cost or maximize gain).

Examples: Minimize expenses, maximize profit, maximize productivity, minimize waiting time.

Worked Example

Objective: Maximize the number of computers (x)
Subject to constraint: 25,000x ≤ 300,000 → x ≤ 12.

Summary of the Four Components

Component Easy Meaning Practical Example
Decision Variable What we choose / control Number of computers to purchase
Parameter Information already given Price per computer (₱25,000)
Constraint Our limitation / ceiling Available budget (₱300,000)
Objective Function What we want to achieve Buy as many computers as possible

Part 3

DECISION-MAKING UNDER CERTAINTY, RISK, AND UNCERTAINTY

These conditions describe how much reliable information we have regarding future outcomes.

3.1 Decision-Making Under Certainty

Certainty means the relevant outcome is known with 100% confidence.

Example: Keyboards Purchase

You purchase 5 keyboards at ₱500 each.

5 × ₱500 = ₱2,500. There is no guessing or probability involved—both quantity and price are fixed and known.

3.2 Decision-Making Under Risk

Risk means we know the possible outcomes and can assign reliable probabilities to them based on historical records or statistical evidence.

Example: Product Demand

A computer shop estimates two demand situations for keyboards:

  • High demand: 70% probability
  • Low demand: 30% probability

We know the possible situations and their odds, but we do not know which specific outcome will actually occur.

3.3 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Uncertainty means we do not have enough historical or reliable data to assign objective probabilities to possible outcomes.

Example: Brand New Software Launch

A company launches an innovative new software product with zero historical sales data. While they anticipate high, medium, or low demand, they cannot reliably state that high demand has a 70% chance.

Comparison Matrix

Condition Explanation Real-World Example
Certainty Outcome is known and deterministic Fixed computer purchase cost under signed contract
Risk Possible outcomes & probabilities are known Estimated product demand based on last year's sales
Uncertainty Probabilities cannot be reliably determined Launching a revolutionary tech product with no prior history
👨‍🏫
Teacher Explanation:
"Remember: Certainty means we know the outcome. Risk means we have possible outcomes and probabilities. Uncertainty means we do not have enough information to calculate reliable probabilities."
Part 4

DETERMINISTIC APPROACHES

4.1 What is a Deterministic Approach?

A deterministic approach is a method of solving problems where the inputs are treated as known and fixed.

In simple English: We already know the values, so we use them to calculate the exact answer.

For identical inputs and assumptions, a deterministic model will always produce the identical output.

4.2 Example 1: Calculating Total Expenses

A school wants to purchase equipment as shown below:

Equipment Quantity Price per Unit Subtotal
Keyboard 20 ₱400 ₱8,000
Mouse 20 ₱250 ₱5,000
Monitor 10 ₱5,000 ₱50,000
Total Equipment Expense ₱63,000

Step-by-step breakdown: ₱8,000 + ₱5,000 + ₱50,000 = ₱63,000.

4.3 Example 2: Calculating Profit

You sell 20 keyboards at a selling price of ₱800 each. The cost of purchasing each keyboard is ₱500.

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost
Method 1: Total Revenue Minus Total Cost
Step 1: Revenue = ₱800 × 20 = ₱16,000
Step 2: Total Cost = ₱500 × 20 = ₱10,000
Step 3: Profit = ₱16,000 − ₱10,000
Profit = ₱6,000
Method 2: Profit per Item First
Step 1: Profit per unit = ₱800 − ₱500 = ₱300
Step 2: Total profit = ₱300 × 20 units
Profit = ₱6,000

4.4 Example 3: Calculating Percentage

A class has 40 students. Thirty students passed the exam. What percentage of students passed?

Percentage = (Number of Students Passed ÷ Total Students) × 100
Calculation

(30 ÷ 40) × 100 = 0.75 × 100 = 75%

75% of the class passed.

4.5 Example 4: Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis determines the volume of activity where total revenue exactly equals total costs (Profit = ₱0).

Break-Even Quantity = Fixed Cost ÷ (Selling Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit)

Shop Scenario:

  • Fixed Cost: ₱10,000 / month (Rent, base utilities)
  • Service Charge (Price): ₱700 / customer
  • Variable Cost: ₱200 / customer (Materials per repair)
Break-Even Steps
1: Contribution margin per customer: ₱700 − ₱200 = ₱500
2: Break-Even Quantity: ₱10,000 ÷ ₱500 = 20 customers
Break-Even Point = 20 Customers
Verification at 20 Customers

Revenue: 20 × ₱700 = ₱14,000
Variable Cost: 20 × ₱200 = ₱4,000
Fixed Cost: ₱10,000
Total Cost: ₱10,000 + ₱4,000 = ₱14,000
Profit: ₱14,000 − ₱14,000 = ₱0 (Break-Even achieved!)

What happens beyond break-even? If the shop serves 25 customers:
Revenue = 25 × ₱700 = ₱17,500 | Total Cost = ₱10,000 + (25 × ₱200) = ₱15,000 | Profit = ₱2,500.

Advantages & Limitations of Deterministic Approaches

✅ Advantages
  • Easier to calculate and understand
  • Ideal when input values are known and fixed
  • Produces consistent, repeatable results
⚠️ Limitations
  • Does not explicitly represent uncertain outcomes
  • Unexpected changes alter real-world results
  • Faulty input values produce misleading conclusions
Part 5

PROBABILISTIC APPROACHES

5.1 What is a Probabilistic Approach?

A probabilistic approach is a method of solving problems that incorporates probability to analyze uncertain situations.

In simple English: We do not know exactly what will happen, so we calculate the likelihood of different possible outcomes.

Example: A repair shop estimates daily customer arrivals from historical patterns: 5 customers (20% chance), 10 customers (50% chance), and 15 customers (30% chance).

5.2 Understanding Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is to happen. It can be expressed as a decimal (0.50), percentage (50%), or fraction (1/2).

Probability Qualitative Meaning
0%Impossible under given conditions
25%Low chance / unlikely
50%Equal chance in a 2-outcome situation
75%High chance / likely
100%Certain under given conditions

5.3 Basic Probability Formula

Probability = Number of Favorable Outcomes ÷ Total Number of Equally Likely Outcomes
Example 1: Selecting a Working Computer Cable

A box contains 10 cables: 7 working and 3 defective.

Step 1: Favorable outcomes (working) = 7
Step 2: Total outcomes = 10
Step 3: 7 ÷ 10 = 0.70 = 70%
Probability of selecting a working cable = 70%
Example 2: Selecting a Defective Cable

Defective cables = 3 out of 10 total cables.

• 3 ÷ 10 = 0.30 = 30%
Probability of selecting a defective cable = 30%

Verification: 70% + 30% = 100% (Covers all exhaustive possible states).

Part 6

EXPECTED MONETARY VALUE (EMV)

6.1 What is EMV?

Expected Monetary Value (EMV) is used to compare decisions by calculating the weighted average of their possible monetary outcomes based on probabilities.

In simple English: EMV helps us estimate the average financial payoff of a decision across multiple uncertain outcomes.
EMV = (Probability 1 × Value 1) + (Probability 2 × Value 2) + ... + (Probability n × Value n)
📌
Crucial Rule: Always convert percentages to decimals before multiplying (e.g. 70% = 0.70, 30% = 0.30).

6.2 Worked Example 1: Choosing Inventory for a Store

A store owner must decide whether to order a Small or Large Inventory of keyboards.

Inventory Decision High Demand (60% / 0.60) Low Demand (40% / 0.40)
Small Inventory ₱30,000 ₱25,000
Large Inventory ₱50,000 ₱10,000
EMV Step-by-Step Calculation
Small: (0.60 × ₱30,000) + (0.40 × ₱25,000) = ₱18,000 + ₱10,000 = ₱28,000
Large: (0.60 × ₱50,000) + (0.40 × ₱10,000) = ₱30,000 + ₱4,000 = ₱34,000
Diff: ₱34,000 − ₱28,000 = ₱6,000 in favor of Large Inventory
Final Recommendation: Large Inventory has the higher EMV by ₱6,000.
💡
Does EMV Guarantee ₱34,000?
No! The store will either experience high demand (making ₱50,000) or low demand (making ₱10,000). EMV represents the long-run expected average value across repeated trials under these probabilities.

6.3 Worked Example 2: Repair Shop Promotional Strategy

Compare two marketing options under High Demand (70% / 0.70) and Low Demand (30% / 0.30):

Strategy Option High Demand Profit Low Demand Profit EMV Calculation Final EMV
Option A: Basic Promo ₱20,000 ₱8,000 (0.70 × 20k) + (0.30 × 8k) ₱16,400
Option B: Major Promo ₱35,000 ₱2,000 (0.70 × 35k) + (0.30 × 2k) ₱25,100

Result: Option B provides an EMV advantage of ₱8,700 (₱25,100 − ₱16,400). However, because Option B only earns ₱2,000 in low demand, the decision-maker must weigh risk tolerance.

Part 7

PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTIONS

7.1 What is a Probability Distribution?

A probability distribution is a table or mathematical rule showing all mutually exclusive outcomes and their associated probabilities.

Number of Laptops for Repair Probability
20.20 (20%)
40.50 (50%)
60.30 (30%)
Sum of All Probabilities (ΣP) 1.00 (100%)
⭐
Fundamental Validity Rule:
The sum of all individual probabilities in a complete discrete probability distribution must strictly equal 1.00 (or 100%).

What If the Total Is Not 100%?

If probabilities sum to 0.20 + 0.50 + 0.20 = 0.90 (90%), there is an unaccounted 10% probability gap. The distribution is incomplete and must be verified before making critical business decisions.

Part 8

DETERMINISTIC VS. PROBABILISTIC APPROACHES

Let's contrast both approaches side-by-side using the same school laboratory context (30 computers):

Situation A: Deterministic

Every computer receives scheduled maintenance at a known fixed fee of ₱500 per unit.

30 computers × ₱500 = ₱15,000

Total Known Cost: ₱15,000

Situation B: Probabilistic

Maintenance is demand-based and uncertain:

  • 5 units: 20% (1.0)
  • 10 units: 50% (5.0)
  • 15 units: 30% (4.5)

Expected units = 10.5
10.5 × ₱500 = ₱5,250

Expected Cost: ₱5,250
Dimension Deterministic Approach Probabilistic Approach
Core Assumption The quantity/value is known and fixed The quantity is uncertain; modeled with probabilities
Calculation Nature Direct multiplication / algebra Weighted probability expectations (EMV)
Output Result Fixed known amount (e.g. ₱15,000) Expected statistical amount (e.g. ₱5,250)
💡
Quick Memory Aid:
Deterministic: "I know the numbers with certainty, so I calculate the exact answer."
Probabilistic: "I don't know exactly what will happen, so I use likelihoods to estimate expected outcomes."
Part 9

QUANTITATIVE PROBLEM-SOLVING FRAMEWORKS

9.1 What is a Problem-Solving Framework?

A problem-solving framework is a disciplined, step-by-step roadmap that guides decision-makers from problem identification to sustained implementation.

1. Problem → 2. Objective → 3. Data → 4. Model → 5. Solution → 6. Evaluation → 7. Implementation

9.2 The Seven Steps in Detail

Step 1: Define the Problem

Clearly specify the issue. Example: "The computer lab has slow internet and suffers network congestion."

Step 2: Identify the Objective

Define the measurable desired end-state. Example: "Ensure smooth student access to online educational resources without lag."

Step 3: Collect Relevant Data

Gather empirical figures (40 computers, 5 Mbps/computer, 100 Mbps current bandwidth, 200 Mbps proposed).

Step 4: Formulate a Mathematical Model

Construct the formula: Required Bandwidth = Number of Computers × Bandwidth per Unit = 40 × 5 = 200 Mbps.

Step 5: Solve the Model

Perform the calculation: Deficit = 200 Mbps − 100 Mbps = 100 Mbps additional capacity needed.

Step 6: Evaluate and Validate the Result

Critique assumptions: Do all 40 computers stream high bandwidth simultaneously? Monitor actual traffic before spending funds.

Step 7: Implement and Monitor

Execute the chosen upgrade and continually observe network metrics, latency, and student feedback.

Part 10

DECISION-MAKING USING WEIGHTED SCORING

When multiple competing criteria (e.g. price, performance, durability) matter, the Weighted Scoring Model enables objective evaluation.

Weighted Score = Σ (Weight × Criterion Rating)

Example: Selecting a Programming Laptop

You evaluate 3 laptops across 3 criteria with ratings from 1 (Very Poor) to 5 (Excellent):

Evaluation Criteria Assigned Weight Laptop A Laptop B Laptop C
Price (Affordability) 0.40 (40%) 5 3 4
Performance 0.40 (40%) 3 5 4
Battery Life 0.20 (20%) 4 3 5
Weight Sum & Total Scores 1.00 (100%) 4.00 3.80 4.20
Calculation Breakdown
A: (0.40 × 5) + (0.40 × 3) + (0.20 × 4) = 2.00 + 1.20 + 0.80 = 4.00
B: (0.40 × 3) + (0.40 × 5) + (0.20 × 3) = 1.20 + 2.00 + 0.60 = 3.80
C: (0.40 × 4) + (0.40 × 4) + (0.20 × 5) = 1.60 + 1.60 + 1.00 = 4.20
Winner: Laptop C achieves the highest weighted score (4.20).

Calculate the EMV of both and determine which decision has higher expected monetary value.
Reveal Answer & Working

Option A: (0.60 × ₱25,000) + (0.40 × ₱15,000) = ₱15,000 + ₱6,000 = ₱21,000

Option B: (0.60 × ₱40,000) + (0.40 × ₱5,000) = ₱24,000 + ₱2,000 = ₱26,000

Option B has the higher EMV by ₱5,000 (₱26,000 vs. ₱21,000).

FINAL LESSON SUMMARY

Before concluding this module, ensure you master these five central pillars:

1. Quantitative Analysis: Using numbers, data, and systematic calculation to understand problems and guide optimal choices.
2. Four Model Components: Decision Variables (controllable choices), Parameters (given values), Constraints (limitations), and Objective Function (goals to maximize or minimize).
3. Three Decision Conditions: Certainty (outcomes known), Risk (outcomes & probabilities known), and Uncertainty (probabilities unknown).
4. Two Approaches: Deterministic (fixed, known inputs) vs. Probabilistic (likelihoods, distributions, and EMV under uncertainty).
5. Seven-Step Framework: Problem → Objective → Data → Model → Solution → Evaluation → Implementation.

Part 1 • Understanding Quantitative Analysis

What is Quantitative Analysis?

Before jumping into formulas, let's look at what the two words mean:

Quantitative

Something that can be measured, counted, or expressed in numbers (e.g. price, quantity, speed, time).

Analysis

Examining information carefully to understand a situation, make comparisons, or solve a problem.

Formal Definition: The process of using numbers, data, mathematical calculations, and logical methods to understand problems and make decisions.
"In simple English: Quantitative Analysis means using numbers to help us make better decisions."
Teacher Notes
Emphasize that qualitative analysis looks at non-numeric traits (like color or brand popularity), whereas quantitative analysis brings exact numerical rigor.
Part 1 • Practical Examples

Example 1: Buying a Programming Laptop

Imagine you are a student choosing a computer for your programming classes:

Computer ModelPrice

Computer B₱30,000

  • Lesson: Numbers allow us to compare both costs and specifications before reaching a sound decision.
Teacher Notes
Ask students: Who would buy A? Who would buy B? Point out that price alone isn't the whole story—quantitative metrics must evaluate value, not just lowest tag.
Part 1 • Practical Examples

Example 2: Calculating Class Average Score

A teacher records examination scores for five students: 80, 90, 85, 75, 70.

Average = Total Scores ÷ Number of Students
Step 1 Add all scores: 80 + 90 + 85 + 75 + 70 = 400
Step 2 Count total students: 5 students
Step 3 Divide total by count: 400 ÷ 5 = 80

Result: The class average is 80.

Why divide by 5? To distribute the total points equally across all students, providing a single benchmark number representing group performance.

Teacher Notes
Ask the class: What does the average tell the teacher? (Whether the exam was too hard, just right, or if the class grasped the module).

Part 2 • Decision-Making Models

What is a Decision-Making Model?

Decision-making is selecting one option from several alternatives (e.g. which ISP to hire, how many PCs to order, which project to fund).

Definition: A Decision-Making Model is a simplified mathematical representation of a real-world problem that helps us compare alternatives and choose an optimal course of action.

Why do we build models?

  • Real-world problems are too complex to keep in our heads.
  • Models isolate the essential facts (costs, limits, goals).
  • They allow us to test "what-if" scenarios before spending actual money.
Teacher Notes
Analogy: An architectural blueprint is a model of a house. A mathematical decision model is a blueprint of a business decision.

Part 2 • Model Components

The 4 Basic Components of a Decision Model

Every quantitative decision model consists of four fundamental building blocks:

ComponentEasy MeaningLaboratory Example
1. Decision VariableWhat we control / decideNumber of computers to buy (x)
2. ParameterInformation given / fixedPrice per computer (₱25,000)
3. ConstraintLimitation or restrictionTotal budget ceiling (₱300,000)
4. Objective FunctionWhat we want to achieveMaximize total computers purchased
Teacher Notes
Ensure students memorize these four terms: Variables, Parameters, Constraints, Objective. They appear in every business and IT optimization model.
Part 2 • Model Components

Variables vs. Parameters

A. Decision Variable (x)

Something the decision-maker directly chooses.

  • Quantity of computers to buy
  • Number of staff to schedule
  • Price to charge customers

If school buys 10 PCs, x = 10; if 15, x = 15. The value is in your control.

B. Parameters (c)

Known, given, or estimated values taken as fixed.

  • Hardware price (₱25,000 per PC)
  • Monthly electrical utility rate
  • Taxes or supplier contract rates
Cost = x · c = 10 × ₱25,000 = ₱250,000
Teacher Notes
Key distinction: You decide the Variable. The market or environment gives you the Parameter.
Part 2 • Model Components

Constraints & Objective Function

C. Constraints (Limits)

Boundaries that our decision cannot violate.

Budget = ₱300,000 | Price = ₱25,000
Max PCs = ₱300,000 ÷ ₱25,000 = 12 PCs

Buying 13 PCs costs ₱325,000 (exceeds budget by ₱25,000). The budget is our constraint!

D. Objective Function (Goal)

The mathematical goal we want to achieve:

  • Maximize: Profit, efficiency, units purchased
  • Minimize: Costs, waiting time, defect rate
Maximize x subject to: 25,000x ≤ 300,000
Teacher Notes
Emphasize the inequality: 25,000x ≤ 300,000. Real-world decisions always operate within limits (money, time, storage).

Part 3 • Decision-Making Conditions

Certainty, Risk, and Uncertainty

How much information do we possess about future outcomes?

1. Certainty

Relevant outcome is known with 100% confidence.

Buying 5 keyboards @ ₱500 = exactly ₱2,500. No probabilities needed.

2. Risk

Outcomes are uncertain, but probabilities are known.

High Demand: 70%, Low Demand: 30% based on historical sales data.

3. Uncertainty

Outcomes possible, but probabilities cannot be determined.

Launching a novel AI product with zero historical records.

Teacher Notes
Emphasize: Risk has numbers (probabilities). Uncertainty has no numbers. Certainty has fixed numbers.
Part 3 • Decision Conditions

Conditions Comparison Matrix

ConditionCore ExplanationReal-Life Example
Certainty Outcome is completely known Fixed computer purchase cost under signed contract
Risk Possible outcomes & probabilities are known Estimated product demand based on past 3 years of sales
Uncertainty Probabilities cannot be reliably computed Launching a completely new software with no market history
👨‍🏫 Teacher Memory Cue:
"Certainty = We know.
Risk = We calculate odds.
Uncertainty = We have no reliable odds."
Teacher Notes
Ask students: Which condition do businesses prefer? (Certainty is easiest, but profit opportunities often live in managing Risk).
Part 4 • Deterministic Approaches

What is a Deterministic Approach?

Definition: A problem-solving method where all inputs, costs, and parameters are treated as known and fixed.
"We already know the values, so we use them to calculate the exact answer."

Key properties of deterministic models:

  • No randomness or probability involved.
  • For identical inputs, the calculation always produces the identical output.
  • Example: Buying 10 mice @ ₱250 = 10 × ₱250 = ₱2,500.
Teacher Notes
Deterministic comes from "determine"—the inputs determine the exact single result.
Part 4 • Deterministic Example 1

Calculating Total Equipment Expenses

A school purchases laboratory accessories with fixed prices:

EquipmentQuantityPrice per UnitSubtotal
Keyboard20₱400₱8,000
Mouse20₱250₱5,000
Monitor10₱5,000₱50,000
Total Laboratory Expense ₱63,000

Step-by-step logic: Calculate each item subtotal (Quantity × Unit Price), then sum subtotals: ₱8,000 + ₱5,000 + ₱50,000 = ₱63,000.

Teacher Notes
Point out how standard invoices and procurement receipts are deterministic calculations.
Part 4 • Deterministic Example 2

Calculating Business Profit

You sell 20 keyboards. Selling Price = ₱800 each. Supplier Cost = ₱500 each.

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost

Method 1: Totals

Revenue: 20 × ₱800 = ₱16,000

Cost: 20 × ₱500 = ₱10,000

Profit: ₱16,000 − ₱10,000 = ₱6,000

Method 2: Per-Unit Margin

Margin / item: ₱800 − ₱500 = ₱300

Total units: 20 keyboards

Profit: 20 × ₱300 = ₱6,000

Both methods verify the same conclusion: ₱6,000 Net Profit.

Teacher Notes
Remind students of the definitions: Revenue = money coming in; Cost = money going out; Profit = net remaining money.
Part 4 • Deterministic Example 3

Calculating Percentages

In a class of 40 students, 30 students passed the examination. What percentage passed?

Percentage = (Number of Students Passed ÷ Total Students) × 100
Step 1 Divide passed by total: 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75
Step 2 Multiply by 100: 0.75 × 100 = 75%
Why multiply by 100? "Percent" means per hundred. The decimal 0.75 represents 75 parts out of every 100.
Teacher Notes
Percentages normalize data so you can compare classes of different sizes (e.g. 30/40 vs 45/60).
Part 4 • Break-Even Analysis

Understanding Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis finds the exact activity volume where Total Revenue = Total Costs (Profit = ₱0).

Break-Even Quantity = Fixed Cost ÷ (Selling Price − Variable Cost)

Fixed Cost

Does NOT change with output volume (e.g. shop rent: ₱10,000/mo).

Service Charge

Price collected from each customer (e.g. ₱700 / repair).

Variable Cost

Cost that scales per unit (e.g. thermal paste & parts: ₱200 / customer).

Teacher Notes
Emphasize: Selling price minus variable cost (₱700 - ₱200 = ₱500) is the "Contribution Margin" that pays off rent.
Part 4 • Break-Even Analysis

Break-Even Calculation & Verification

Step 1 Contribution Margin: ₱700 − ₱200 = ₱500 per customer
Step 2 Divide Fixed Cost by Margin: ₱10,000 ÷ ₱500 = 20 customers
Verification at 20 Customers:
Revenue = 20 × ₱700 = ₱14,000
Total Costs = ₱10,000 (fixed) + (20 × ₱200 variable) = ₱14,000
Profit = ₱14,000 − ₱14,000 = ₱0 (Break-Even Verified!)

Beyond Break-Even: If you serve 25 customers, profit is 5 × ₱500 = ₱2,500 net profit!

Teacher Notes
Remind students: Fewer than 20 customers = loss. Exactly 20 = break even. More than 20 = profit.
Part 4 • Deterministic Evaluation

Strengths & Limitations of Deterministic Models

✅ Advantages

  • Straightforward to calculate and explain
  • Clear and transparent logic
  • Highly dependable when inputs are fixed
  • Consistent results every time

⚠️ Limitations

  • Cannot model real-world uncertainty
  • Assumes conditions never fluctuate unexpectedly
  • Garbage In, Garbage Out: Faulty inputs produce faulty answers
Teacher Notes
Transition slide: "What happens when inputs are NOT fixed? That brings us to Probabilistic Approaches."
Part 5 • Probabilistic Approaches

What is a Probabilistic Approach?

Definition: A problem-solving method that uses probabilities to model and analyze uncertain future events.
"We do not know exactly what will happen, so we calculate the likelihood of different possible outcomes."

Real-World Repair Shop Example:

  • 5 customer arrivals tomorrow: 20% chance (0.20)
  • 10 customer arrivals tomorrow: 50% chance (0.50)
  • 15 customer arrivals tomorrow: 30% chance (0.30)

We don't know the exact count tomorrow, but past records provide valuable probabilities to plan staffing and inventory!

Teacher Notes
Contrast this with deterministic: Deterministic says "exactly 10 will arrive". Probabilistic says "there's a distribution of likelihoods".
Part 5 • Probability Basics

Probability Scale & Basic Formula

Probability = Favorable Outcomes ÷ Total Possible Outcomes
ValueQualitative MeaningExample
0% (0.00)ImpossibleA standard coin landing on 7
25% (0.25)Low chanceRain on a dry-season morning
50% (0.50)Equal chance (even odds)Fair coin flip landing on heads
75% (0.75)High chanceExperienced student passing test
100% (1.00)CertainThe sun rising tomorrow
Teacher Notes
Emphasize: Probability is always bounded between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%). It can never be negative, and never exceed 100%.
Part 5 • Probability Examples

Selecting Working vs. Defective Cables

A storage bin contains 10 cables: 7 working and 3 defective.

A. Working Cable Probability

Favorable = 7 | Total = 10

P(Working) = 7 ÷ 10 = 0.70 = 70%

B. Defective Cable Probability

Favorable = 3 | Total = 10

P(Defective) = 3 ÷ 10 = 0.30 = 30%
Verification: 70% + 30% = 100% (1.00).
Because every cable is either working or defective, the sum of all mutually exclusive probabilities equals 100%.
Teacher Notes
Ask students: If you draw 20 random cables from this batch, how many do you expect to work? (20 × 0.70 = 14 cables).
Part 6 • Expected Monetary Value (EMV)

What is Expected Monetary Value (EMV)?

Definition: EMV calculates the weighted average financial outcome of a decision by weighting each possible payoff by its probability of occurrence.
EMV = (Probability 1 × Value 1) + (Probability 2 × Value 2) + ... + (P_n × V_n)

Crucial Rule: Always convert percentages to decimals before multiplying!

  • 60% = 0.60
  • 40% = 0.40
  • 70% = 0.70
Teacher Notes
Emphasize: EMV is the standard decision criterion used by corporations, banks, and IT directors to evaluate risk.
Part 6 • EMV Worked Example 1

Inventory Decision: Small vs. Large Stock

Demand states: High Demand (60% / 0.60) vs. Low Demand (40% / 0.40)

Inventory DecisionHigh Demand (60%)Low Demand (40%)Calculated EMV
Small Inventory ₱30,000 ₱25,000 ₱28,000
Large Inventory ₱50,000 ₱10,000 ₱34,000
Small (0.60 × ₱30k) + (0.40 × ₱25k) = ₱18,000 + ₱10,000 = ₱28,000
Large (0.60 × ₱50k) + (0.40 × ₱10k) = ₱30,000 + ₱4,000 = ₱34,000

Conclusion: Large Inventory has the higher EMV by ₱6,000 (₱34,000 vs. ₱28,000).

Teacher Notes
Walk through the math carefully. Make sure students see how ₱18k + ₱10k gives ₱28k.
Part 6 • EMV Deep Dive

Why Multiply Probabilities by Payoffs?

Why can't we just add ₱50,000 + ₱10,000 = ₱60,000?

Because high demand and low demand cannot happen at the same time! They are mutually exclusive outcomes.

By weighting each outcome by its likelihood, we obtain a single statistically valid metric that allows fair comparison between alternatives.

Does Large Inventory Guarantee ₱34,000?

NO! The store will either make ₱50,000 (if high demand) or ₱10,000 (if low demand). ₱34,000 is the expected long-run average across repeated cycles.

Teacher Notes
Crucial distinction: EMV is an expected value, not a guaranteed return. Make sure students don't confuse expectation with promise.
Part 6 • EMV Worked Example 2

Promotion Strategy: Basic vs. Major

Shop marketing campaign: High Demand = 70% (0.70) | Low Demand = 30% (0.30)

Option A: Basic Promo

High: ₱20,000 | Low: ₱8,000

EMV = (0.70 × 20k) + (0.30 × 8k)
= 14,000 + 2,400 = ₱16,400

Option B: Major Promo

High: ₱35,000 | Low: ₱2,000

EMV = (0.70 × 35k) + (0.30 × 2k)
= 24,500 + 600 = ₱25,100
Result: Major Promo has an EMV advantage of ₱8,700 (₱25,100 − ₱16,400).
Trade-off Insight: Option B yields only ₱2,000 in low demand. The manager must consider their risk tolerance!
Teacher Notes
Introduce the concept of risk aversion: If the business has cash flow issues and needs at least ₱5,000 to survive, they might choose Option A despite lower EMV.
Part 6 • Expected Values

Expected Non-Monetary Quantities

Expected values don't just apply to money—they predict traffic, users, and customer arrivals:

Daily Customers (x)Probability P(x)Weighted Product [x · P(x)]
5 customers0.201.0
10 customers0.505.0
15 customers0.304.5
Expected Daily Customers (Sum) 10.5 customers / day

Meaning: This does not mean "half a human" will arrive! It means that across hundreds of operating days, the average daily volume converges to 10.5 customers.

Teacher Notes
Students often ask: "Can you have 0.5 of a customer?" Address this clearly: Expectation is a mathematical average.
Part 7 • Probability Distributions

Probability Distributions & The 100% Rule

A probability distribution displays all mutually exclusive outcomes and their individual probabilities.

Laptops Received for RepairProbability
2 laptops0.20 (20%)
4 laptops0.50 (50%)
6 laptops0.30 (30%)
Total Probability (ΣP) 1.00 (100%)
⭐ The Golden Rule: The sum of all probabilities in a complete discrete probability distribution MUST equal exactly 1.00 (100%).
If probabilities sum to 0.90 (90%), a 10% outcome is missing and the model is invalid!
Teacher Notes
Emphasize this check: Always sum the probabilities first. If ΣP ≠ 1.0, stop and find the missing state.
Part 8 • Synthesis & Comparison

Lab Maintenance: Two Approaches

Consider a school laboratory with 30 computers under two different maintenance regimes:

Situation A: Deterministic

Fixed annual contract: Every single PC receives scheduled service at ₱500.

30 PCs × ₱500 = ₱15,000
Fixed Known Cost: ₱15,000

Situation B: Probabilistic

On-demand maintenance: Only broken PCs are serviced (₱500/repair):

  • 5 units (20%) = 1.0 | 10 (50%) = 5.0 | 15 (30%) = 4.5
Expected units = 10.5 PCs
10.5 × ₱500 = ₱5,250 Expected Cost
Teacher Notes
Note: These two examples describe different business policies: Universal maintenance vs on-demand repair.

Part 9 • Problem-Solving Frameworks

The 7-Step Quantitative Framework

When facing a technical problem, never jump immediately to spending money. Follow the 7-step sequence:

1. PROBLEM  →  2. OBJECTIVE  →  3. DATA  →  4. MODEL
↓
5. SOLUTION  →  6. EVALUATION  →  7. IMPLEMENTATION

Analogy: When a computer runs slow, you don't instantly throw it out. You define the issue, measure RAM/storage, diagnose the bottleneck, test a fix, and observe results.

Teacher Notes
Emphasize Step 6 (Evaluation) and Step 7 (Implementation). Many novice analysts stop at Step 5 and produce unworkable recommendations.
Part 9 • The 7 Steps in Detail

Deep Dive: Laboratory Internet Case

1. Problem: Lab internet is sluggish and drops connections.

2. Objective: Deliver smooth, non-congested student browsing.

3. Data: 40 PCs, 5 Mbps/PC, 100 Mbps current bandwidth.

4. Model: Req Bandwidth = 40 × 5 Mbps = 200 Mbps.

5. Solution: Deficit = 200 − 100 = 100 Mbps deficit.

6. Evaluation: Do all 40 PCs stream HD video at the same second? Measure actual usage before upgrading!

7. Implement & Monitor: Upgrade to 200 Mbps and track latency, downtime, and satisfaction.

Teacher Notes
Highlight Step 6: What if the problem wasn't bandwidth, but a faulty router switch? Evaluation prevents wasteful spending.
Part 10 • Multi-Criteria Decisions

Decision-Making Using Weighted Scoring

Real-world decisions rarely depend on price alone. We often evaluate multiple competing criteria.

Weighted Score = (Weight 1 × Rating 1) + (Weight 2 × Rating 2) + ...

Rules for Criteria Weights:

  • Criteria weights reflect relative importance.
  • The sum of all criteria weights must equal 1.00 (or 100%).
  • Items are rated on a standard scale (e.g. 1 = Very Poor, 5 = Excellent).
Teacher Notes
Explain that if a student values battery life more than gaming graphics, their weights will differ from a gamer's weights.

  • Highest raw benefit score (10)

Part 16 • Lesson Summary

The 5 Core Pillars of Quantitative Analysis

1. Quantitative Analysis: Using numbers, data, and logic to understand problems and decide wisely.
2. 4 Model Components: Decision Variables (choices), Parameters (given values), Constraints (limits), Objective (goal).
3. 3 Decision Conditions: Certainty (known), Risk (probabilities known), Uncertainty (probabilities unknown).
4. 2 Core Approaches: Deterministic (fixed, repeatable values) vs. Probabilistic (likelihoods, distributions, EMV).
5. 7-Step Framework: Problem → Objective → Data → Model → Solution → Evaluation → Implementation.

Final Takeaway

Final Message to Students

"Class, remember that quantitative analysis is not just about solving mathematical problems. It is about learning how to make better decisions.

When you buy a laptop, manage a budget, select an internet plan, or troubleshoot a computer system, you are already dealing with decisions that can be supported by numbers.

Always remember: Do not just calculate the answer. Understand what the answer tells you and how it can help you make a better decision."
